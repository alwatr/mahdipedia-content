Introduction

Belief in the promised Mahdi (may Allah hasten his noble reappearance) in Islamic culture is not merely a belief concerning the future. It can also play a significant role in shaping human individual and social conduct. The anticipation of justice, the rejection of oppression, a sense of responsibility, hope for the future, and the pursuit of social reform are among the most important social capacities embedded in this belief.

Yet this very social potential, when detached from sound religious and intellectual standards, can become a point of vulnerability and a means of exploitation. History has shown that certain individuals or movements have attempted to attribute themselves to the “Mahdi,” claim to be his “representative” or “messenger,” or assert that they possess a special relationship with him, in order to establish legitimacy, attract followers, acquire power, or gain social influence.

The central question of this article, therefore, has two dimensions:

First: What healthy and constructive functions can belief in the Mahdi (may Allah hasten his noble reappearance) serve within society?

Second: How can this very belief be transformed by claimants to Mahdi-hood into an instrument of deception and social domination?

In this article, we will focus on the first of these two dimensions.

1. Mahdism: A Personal Belief or a Social Capital?

If we reduce Mahdism merely to the proposition that “a person will appear in the future and reform the world,” we overlook a significant part of its social implications.

The idea of awaiting the Mahdi can free a person from indifference toward the fate of society.

A society that regards itself as awaiting justice cannot remain indifferent to oppression, corruption, discrimination, falsehood, betrayal, or the violation of the rights of others.

From this perspective, genuine anticipation does not mean putting life on hold until the time of the Mahdi’s reappearance. Rather, it means cultivating human beings who are capable of living in a just society and who, within the limits of their abilities, strive to move society toward reform.

Viewed in this light, Mahdism can fulfill several important social functions:

A) Cultivating Hope

A human society deprived of hope gradually becomes worn down by its difficulties. Belief in a better future can prevent the problems of the present from turning into a form of historical despair.

B) Strengthening Social Responsibility

If justice is regarded as a value, then one who awaits justice cannot at the same time become an agent of oppression.

If Imam al-Mahdi (may Allah hasten his noble reappearance) is presented as an embodiment of justice, then awaiting him should also leave its mark upon a person’s individual and social conduct.

C) Resisting the Normalization of Oppression

One of the dangers facing societies is that people may gradually become accustomed to corruption and injustice.

The culture of anticipation can keep one fundamental question alive:

“Is the existing state of the world truly the way it ought to be?”

That question, in itself, can become a driving force for social reform.

D) Creating a Shared Identity

A shared belief can bring otherwise dispersed individuals together around a common set of values and principles. Throughout history, Mahdist movements in certain societies have also succeeded in mobilizing diverse social groups. Historical research on Mahdist movements in Africa indicates that, in some cases, Mahdist language and ideology were employed as instruments of social mobilization.

This point, however, carries an important warning:

Not every form of social mobilization carried out in the name of the Mahdi is necessarily Mahdist in its true sense.
