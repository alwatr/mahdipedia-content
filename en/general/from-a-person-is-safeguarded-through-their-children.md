**From “A Person Is Safeguarded Through Their Children” to the Covenant of Supporting Imam al-Mahdi**

**The Responsibilities of the Awaiting Generation in Light of the Words and Conduct of Lady Fatimah al-Zahra**

**Abstract**

Among the thought-provoking statements in Lady Fatimah al-Zahra’s argument after the passing of the Prophet Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him and his family, is a sentence she cited while addressing the Ansar: “Did my father, the Messenger of God, not say: ‘A person is safeguarded through their children’?”

Delivered within a historical and social argument, this statement draws society’s attention to its responsibility toward the dignity, rights, and standing of the Prophet’s child. Reflecting on this statement and other passages of the Fadak sermon makes it possible to derive theological, ethical, educational, and social teachings for a community that awaits the Imam.

In Shi‘i thought, the community’s bond with the Prophet’s Household did not end with the Prophet’s passing; it continued through the line of Imamate. Today, this bond is embodied in the living Imam and divine proof, Imam al-Mahdi, may God hasten his noble reappearance. This article explains the awaiting generation’s responsibilities under eight headings: preserving the bond and covenant with the Imam; obedience and following; unity and solidarity; commitment to the Qur’an; striving for justice; reforming society; patience and steadfastness; and defending the truth. It also examines the family’s role in transmitting Mahdist teachings to the next generation and the place of praying for relief alongside practical preparation.

**Keywords**

Lady Fatimah al-Zahra; the Fadak sermon; the Fadak argument; “A person is safeguarded through their children”; Imam al-Mahdi; waiting; the awaiting generation; Mahdist responsibility; the awaiting family.

**Introduction: From a Historical Statement to a Mahdist Responsibility**

Sometimes a brief statement opens a window onto a major theological and educational truth. Among the words of Lady Fatimah al-Zahra is a statement that can serve as the starting point for reflection on a person’s responsibility toward the Prophet’s Household, especially Imam al-Mahdi, may God hasten his noble reappearance: “Did my father, the Messenger of God, not say: ‘A person is safeguarded through their children’?”

At first glance, this sentence concerns a person’s bond with their child. Yet its place within Lady al-Zahra’s argument gives it a meaning beyond family advice. By recalling it, she placed society before an ethical and social question: How could people who considered themselves lovers and supporters of the Prophet become indifferent, after his passing, to the rights and dignity of his child?

This historical question belongs not only to the past. In Shi‘i belief, the community’s bond with the Prophet’s Household did not end with the Prophet’s passing. It continued through the line of Imamate and continues today in the living Imam and divine proof, Imam al-Mahdi. If the first community was responsible for safeguarding the dignity of the Prophet’s child, what responsibility does today’s community have toward the living Imam from the Prophet’s family? This article seeks to answer that question, moving from Lady al-Zahra’s words to the practical duties of the awaiting generation.

**Part One: The Historical and Theological Foundations of the Relationship with Imam al-Mahdi**

1. The central statement: a word that calls society to responsibility. In her argument with the Ansar after the Prophet’s passing, Lady al-Zahra cited: “Did my father, the Messenger of God, not say: ‘A person is safeguarded through their children’?” The statement is reported in al-Tabarsi’s al-Ihtijaj, in the section describing her address to the Ansar. It must be understood in its historical setting: after the Prophet’s death, Fadak and her rights became matters of dispute, and she reminded society of its responsibilities toward the Prophet and his Household.

She addressed the Ansar as a group with a history of supporting Islam and the Prophet, asking why they remained silent about what had happened to her. In this context, citing the Prophet’s statement about a person being safeguarded through their child reminds the community that the Prophet’s dignity cannot be separated from the dignity of his child and Household. Loyalty to divinely guided figures is not limited to expressions of love; it is also shown by safeguarding their rights, principles, and path.

1. The meaning of “a person is safeguarded through their child”: three layers of one truth. First, preserving dignity: a community’s treatment of the child of a great figure can reveal its respect for that figure. Second, preserving the bond: the community’s responsibility toward a person’s family, legacy, and values does not end when that person passes away. Third, preserving the path and ideals: genuine loyalty is shown by protecting the person’s values and way.

These meanings can provide a basis for a Mahdist analysis. However, the statement itself does not directly refer to Imam al-Mahdi. Connecting it to the Imam of the Age is a theological and educational inference based on the continuity of Imamate and the enduring bond with the Prophet’s Household.

1. From Lady al-Zahra to Imam al-Mahdi: the continuing bond with the Prophet’s Household. According to Twelver Shi‘i belief, Imam al-Mahdi is the son of Imam Hasan al-Askari, a descendant of Imam al-Husayn, and his lineage reaches the Messenger of God and Lady Fatimah al-Zahra. He is therefore not merely a figure belonging to the future, but the living Imam and God’s proof in the present. Connection with the Prophet and Lady al-Zahra cannot, from this perspective, be separated from knowing and remaining loyal to Imam al-Mahdi.
2. Imam al-Mahdi: the living Imam and present divine proof. Occultation does not mean that the Imam does not exist or that Imamate has ended. In Shi‘i belief, Imam al-Mahdi remains the Imam and God’s proof during the Occultation. A reported letter states: “As for the way people benefit from me during my Occultation, it is like benefiting from the sun when clouds conceal it from sight.” Another states: “Indeed, I am a source of security for the people of the earth, just as the stars are a source of security for the people of heaven.” The sun does not cease to be the sun when hidden behind clouds; it simply becomes harder to see. Likewise, Occultation does not mean that the Imam does not exist, but that his visible and public presence is inaccessible.
3. The two-way relationship between the Imam and the community: the Imam has not forgotten us. A reported statement says: “We are not neglectful of your care, nor are we forgetful of remembering you.” The Imam’s care for the Shi‘a does not make the relationship one-sided. We expect his care and prayers, but we also bear responsibilities toward him. In a letter to Shaykh al-Mufid, the Imam states: “If our Shi‘a, may God grant them success in obeying Him, were united in their hearts in fulfilling the covenant incumbent upon them, the blessing of meeting us would not have been delayed for them…” This emphasizes unity of heart and fidelity to the covenant. If our Imam has not forgotten us, have we forgotten our covenant with him?

**Part Two: The Eight Duties of the Awaiting Generation in Light of Lady al-Zahra’s Words**

1. First duty: preserving the bond with the Imam and safeguarding the Mahdist covenant. If preserving the dignity of the Prophet’s child, in the context of Lady al-Zahra’s argument, is a sign of loyalty to the Prophet, the Shi‘i community must preserve its bond with the living proof of the Prophet. This bond has three dimensions: (a) knowledge of the Imam, including the position of Imamate, the Imam’s status as God’s proof, his duties, and his role in guiding society; (b) the Imam’s presence in one’s heart and life, so that outward Occultation does not become forgetfulness; and (c) fidelity to the covenant through religious duties, respect for people’s rights, and commitment to the values of the Prophet’s Household. The relationship must grow from mere emotion into a conscious bond that creates responsibility.
2. Second duty: obedience to the Imam; love must lead to following. In the Fadak sermon, Lady al-Zahra states: “Our obedience is a means of order for the community, and our Imamate is a safeguard against division.” God made obedience to them a means of bringing order to the community and their Imamate a protection against discord. Love for the Imam must therefore lead to following religious teachings and the path of the Prophet’s Household, learning what God has made lawful and unlawful, and avoiding personal preferences or interpretations unsupported by evidence. Love for the Imam is the beginning of the path; obedience is evidence of sincerity in that love.
3. Third duty: unity and solidarity; the awaiting community must not be divided. Imamate is an axis of guidance and social cohesion. During the Occultation, the community should manage differences of opinion and prevent them from turning into hostility and division. The letter of Imam al-Mahdi to Shaykh al-Mufid also emphasizes unity of hearts in fulfilling the covenant. Unity does not mean ignoring the truth or suspending scholarly discussion; it means pursuing the truth ethically and avoiding unnecessary hostility.
4. Fourth duty: commitment to the Qur’an; the standard for recognizing the Imam’s path. Lady al-Zahra describes the Qur’an as “the speaking Book of God, the truthful Qur’an, the shining light, and the brilliant illumination.” She also says that following it leads to divine pleasure and listening to it leads to salvation. The awaiting generation should maintain a daily relationship with the Qur’an, understand and reflect on its verses, learn its teachings on justice and ethics, align its conduct with its guidance, and raise children in the culture of the Qur’an and the Prophet’s Household. Commitment to the Qur’an prevents Mahdist culture from becoming a collection of emotions, unreliable reports, and baseless interpretations.
5. Fifth duty: striving for justice; those who await global justice must begin with themselves. Lady al-Zahra states: “Justice is a means of harmonizing hearts.” Justice is not merely a social rule; it creates trust, peace, and solidarity. The reappearance of Imam al-Mahdi is associated with the spread of justice and the removal of oppression. The awaiting generation should begin by respecting others’ rights within the family, being fair in judgment, avoiding violations of people’s rights, practicing justice at work and in society, and supporting the oppressed. Hope for global justice must not become an excuse for neglecting justice in everyday life.
6. Sixth duty: enjoining good and reforming society. Lady al-Zahra states: “Enjoining what is right is a means of the community’s welfare.” An awaiting community cannot be indifferent to its moral and social future. It should promote good values, invite others to goodness through appropriate words and worthy conduct, remain attentive when oppression becomes normalized, and observe wisdom, kindness, and religious requirements in seeking reform. The one who awaits the reappearance does not merely wish for the world to change, but begins by reforming themselves.
7. Seventh duty: patience and steadfastness. Lady al-Zahra states: “Patience is an aid in becoming deserving of reward.” In religious teaching, patience does not mean accepting oppression or surrendering to deviation. It means remaining firm on the path of truth, preserving faith, and exercising self-control in hardship. The awaiting generation should preserve its faith, never despair of God’s mercy, remain steadfast in religious duties, avoid haste, and maintain hope in divine promises. The Imam’s Occultation must not lead to forgetting him, and the long duration of waiting must not weaken the covenant.
8. Eighth duty: defending the truth; the awaiting believer is not a spectator of history. Lady al-Zahra told the Ansar: “The call reaches you, but you do not respond; the cry for help comes to you, but you do not come to aid.” She questions society’s indifference toward the truth. The lesson is that a good record in the past does not replace responsibility in the present. The awaiting generation should defend the truth through knowledge and reason, support the oppressed, confront lies and distortion, and take responsibility for society. Such defense must be grounded in knowledge, justice, ethics, and respect for others’ rights, not accusation, unfairness, or harmful conduct.

**Part Three: Comparing Two Historical Responsibilities and Educating the Next Generation**

1. From the community of Lady al-Zahra’s time to the community of the Age of Occultation. Revisiting early Islamic history should provide a standard for evaluating our conduct today. After the Prophet’s passing, Lady al-Zahra placed society before its responsibility toward the rights and dignity of the Prophet’s Household. Today, the Shi‘i community in the Age of Occultation faces a different but related responsibility. In that period, the Prophet had passed away and his daughter defended her rights; today, Imam al-Mahdi is alive and God’s proof, but he is in Occultation. This comparison does not equate the two events. It seeks a shared standard of responsibility toward the divine proof and the Prophet’s Household. The earlier community was tested in supporting the truth and protecting the Prophet’s Household; today’s community is tested in knowledge, loyalty, obedience, and readiness to support the Imam.
2. The family: the first foundation for educating the awaiting generation. An important educational implication of “A person is safeguarded through their children” is the family’s responsibility to transmit religious values and beliefs to the next generation. We are responsible not only for raising children, but also for transmitting the covenant of the Prophet’s Household. Mahdist education goes far beyond knowing the Imam’s name or attending religious ceremonies.

We should raise a generation that knows Imam al-Mahdi as the living Imam and divine proof; understands Imamate through knowledge and reasoning; expresses love for the Imam in conduct and lifestyle; knows the duties of the Age of Occultation; learns justice, honesty, trustworthiness, and responsibility; can distinguish claims unsupported by evidence; and understands waiting as moral, intellectual, and social preparation. The home should be the first environment for learning Mahdist culture. Parents’ conduct is more influential than verbal advice alone. Our children should inherit not only the Imam’s name, but knowledge of him, love for him, loyalty to him, and their covenant with him.

1. The supplication for relief: connecting inner expectation with practical readiness. A reported statement says: “Pray often for the hastening of the relief, for indeed that is your relief.” This highlights the importance of prayer, but prayer must not be separated from practical responsibility. A person who prays for global justice should also seek justice in personal life. A person who prays for the Imam’s reappearance should, to the extent of their ability, work to reform themselves and society. Prayer should accompany improved conduct, respect for others’ rights, stronger ethics and spirituality, fulfillment of religious duties, efforts to spread justice and goodness, and the transmission of the culture of waiting to the next generation.

**Part Four: Summary and Final Message**

1. A comprehensive framework of the awaiting generation’s duties in the school of Lady al-Zahra. The eight duties can be summarized as follows: (1) preserving the covenant and bond with the Imam: “A person is safeguarded through their children”; (2) obedience and following: “Our obedience is a means of order for the community”; (3) unity and solidarity: “Our Imamate is a safeguard against division”; (4) commitment to the Qur’an: “the speaking Book of God”; (5) striving for justice: “justice is a means of harmonizing hearts”; (6) reforming society: “enjoining what is right is a means of the community’s welfare”; (7) patience and steadfastness: “patience is an aid in becoming deserving of reward”; and (8) defending the truth, as expressed in Lady al-Zahra’s argument with the Ansar.

These duties portray an awaiting person who bears responsibility on three levels: individual, including knowledge, obedience, commitment to the Qur’an, and patience; social, including unity, justice, reform, and defending the truth; and family and generational, including transmitting knowledge of the Imam, love for him, and loyalty to him to the next generation. Waiting is therefore not passive. It is a conscious and responsible way of life in pursuit of divine ideals.

1. Conclusion: From the Mother’s Solitude to the Child’s Responsibility. At one of the most sensitive moments in Islamic history, Lady Fatimah al-Zahra confronted society with the truth that the Prophet’s passing must not lead the community to forget its responsibility toward his Household. In the context of her argument, “A person is safeguarded through their children” recalls the connection between the Prophet’s dignity and the dignity of his child.

Today, we too face a great responsibility: preserving our bond with the living proof of the Prophet and honoring our covenant with Imam al-Mahdi. This responsibility cannot be fulfilled through verbal expressions of love or attendance at religious ceremonies alone. It requires knowledge, obedience, unity, commitment to the Qur’an, striving for justice, social reform, patience, defense of the truth, and education of the next generation.

We must not speak of Lady al-Zahra’s isolation while neglecting our responsibility toward the living Imam of our time. We must not recall the oppression suffered by the Prophet’s Household while remaining indifferent to truth and justice in our own lives. Nor should we confine love for the Prophet’s Household to emotions and ceremonies; we must turn it into a practical covenant.

Perhaps the most important educational message is that love for Lady al-Zahra should lead us to deeper knowledge of, and greater loyalty to, her descendant, the Master of the Age. Let us read history not merely to recount events, but to learn from it. Let us raise our children to be heirs not only to tears and sorrow, but also to knowledge, insight, faith, and loyalty to Imam al-Mahdi. Let us act so that we can say that during the Occultation we did not merely wait for his arrival; we also tried to honor our covenant with him.

“Let us not honor the mother, yet forget our covenant with her son.”

“O God, hasten the relief of Your representative.”

**Scholarly and Hadith-Critical Notes**

1. Distinguishing the Fadak sermon from the address to the Ansar: The statement “A person is safeguarded through their children” appears in the account of Lady Fatimah al-Zahra’s address to the Ansar within the report of the argument. Without clarification, it should not be presented as part of the original text of her sermon in the mosque.
2. The chain-of-transmission status of the central statement: Its appearance in al-Ihtijaj is not, by itself, sufficient to establish an independently authenticated rijali chain. A definitive judgment requires a separate examination of the report’s chain, different textual versions, and narrator evaluations.
3. Distinguishing transmitted text from inference: In its historical context, the statement concerns society’s responsibility toward the Prophet’s child. Connecting it to the awaiting generation’s responsibility toward Imam al-Mahdi is a theological and educational inference grounded in independent principles of Imamate.
4. The independent status of Mahdist reports: The report comparing the Imam to the sun behind clouds, the letter to Shaykh al-Mufid, and the recommendation to pray for relief are independent narrations cited to complete the Mahdist discussion. They should not be presented as a continuation of Lady al-Zahra’s words.
5. Differences in page numbering: Volume and page numbers in sources such as al-Ihtijaj, Bihar al-Anwar, and Kamal al-Din may vary by publisher and edition. For formal publication, the bibliographic details of the exact edition used should be provided.
